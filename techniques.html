02 — Learn

Winemaking Techniques

Understanding how a wine is made — and why — is the key to selling it confidently. Click any technique to explore.

Winemaking style shapes everything a guest tastes: the bubbles, the texture, the flavor development, the aging potential. When you know the method, you can articulate why a wine costs what it does, how it'll age, and what it pairs with. These are the techniques behind the bottles on our list.

Sparkling

Méthode Champenoise

Double fermentation in the bottle — the gold standard for sparkling wine.

▼

What It Is

Méthode Champenoise (now officially called Méthode Traditionnelle outside Champagne) is the winemaking method that defines the world's greatest sparkling wines. The wine undergoes fermentation twice: once in a tank to become wine, then again in the sealed bottle itself, where the CO₂ produced by yeast gets trapped in the liquid, creating the bubbles and building complexity layer by layer.

The Steps

Base Wine
Grapes are harvested, pressed, and fermented into dry still wine — lower alcohol (11–12%) than table wines, higher acidity. This is the raw material.
Blending (Assemblage)
Multiple base wines from different vintages, vineyards, or even grape varieties are blended to create a consistent house style. This is where the winemaker's art lives.
Tirage
The blended wine is bottled with a small amount of yeast and sugar (liqueur de tirage) added. The bottle is capped with a crown cap (like a beer bottle), sealing it completely.
Second Fermentation
Yeast wakes up and ferments the added sugar, producing alcohol and CO₂. Since the bottle is sealed, the gas can't escape — it dissolves into the wine, creating the bubbles and building pressure to 6–8 atmospheres inside the bottle.
Aging on Lees (Élevage)
The dead yeast cells (lees) settle to the bottom. The wine rests in contact with these lees for months or years (minimum 15 months by law in Champagne, often much longer for prestige cuvées). The lees add nutty, brioche, and toasty complexity — these flavors are the signature of great Champagne.
Riddling (Remuage)
Bottles are placed neck-down in an A-frame rack and rotated slowly over weeks. This slides the compacted lees down toward the neck, collecting them in one spot. Traditional riddling is done by hand; modern producers use automated machines (gyropalettes).
Disgorging (Dégorgement)
The bottle's neck is plunged into an ice-cold brine. The pressure inside freezes a small plug of wine and lees. The crown cap is removed, and the pressure pushes the frozen plug out, along with the trapped lees.
Dosage
A small amount of wine (usually with added sugar — dosage liqueur) is topped back into the bottle to replace what was lost during disgorging and to set the final style. Brut = dry (0–12g sugar/L). Extra Brut = very dry (0–6g). Brut Nature = zero added sugar.
Final Cap & Rest
The bottle is sealed with a cork and wire cage (muselet). Many producers age the finished wine for several more months or years before release, allowing the dosage to integrate.

Why It Matters

Every step is labor-intensive and takes time. This is why méthode Champenoise sparkling wines cost more than mass-produced alternatives. The payoff: finer bubbles (more stable, smaller), deeper complexity from years on the lees, and better aging potential. A great Champagne can develop for 10, 20, or even 50+ years in the bottle.

On Our List

Delamotte (Blanc de Blancs, Millésime, Rosé), Michel Gonet (Les 3 Terroirs), and Laurent-Perrier (Cuvée Rosé, Ultra Brut, Grand Siècle) all use méthode Champenoise. The Avinyó Reserva Brut from Spain uses the same method but can only call itself "Cava" since it's not from Champagne — same rigorous process, different region.

Sparkling

Charmat Method

Second fermentation in a tank, not a bottle — faster, lighter, fresher.

▼

What It Is

Also called "tank method" — the second fermentation happens in a pressurized stainless steel tank instead of individual bottles. CO₂ is still trapped, creating bubbles and pressure, but the wine never ages on yeast cells the way it does in bottles. The result is fresher, crisper, with lighter flavors — and it's ready to sell in weeks instead of years.

Key Differences from Méthode Champenoise

Aspect Méthode Champenoise Charmat
Second Fermentation In bottle In tank
Contact with Lees Months/years Weeks/none
Bubble Size Fine, persistent Larger, dissipates faster
Complexity Nutty, brioche, toasted Fresh, fruity, floral
Time to Market 15 months minimum 4–8 weeks
Price Point Higher Lower

When to Use This Information

Charmat is perfect for Prosecco, light Italian sparklers, and some Moscato — styles designed for immediate drinking with vibrant fruit. If a guest asks why Prosecco costs less than Champagne, this is your answer: different method, different purpose. It's not "worse," it's different.

Sparkling

Pét-Nat (Perlant)

Bottled mid-fermentation — wild, funky, unpredictable.

▼

What It Is

Short for "pétillant naturel" (naturally sparkling). Wine is bottled while still fermenting — yeast is still alive and converting sugar to alcohol. The fermentation continues in the bottle, creating bubbles. It's the oldest method for making sparkling wine (predates Champagne), now experiencing a comeback among natural winemakers.

The Unpredictability

Pét-Nat is alive. No added yeast or sugar. No dosage. The fermentation is wild — unpredictable alcohol levels, variable carbonation, possible bottle variation. Some bottles fizz aggressively; others are nearly still. Some taste funky or off; others are delicious. This is part of its charm and its risk.

Style & Flavor

Pét-Nat is usually low-alcohol (8–11%), fruity, slightly sweet, and often has a cloudy, rustic character — sometimes funky notes of yeast or fermentation that natural winemakers embrace. It's a party wine, not a meditation wine. Think playful, not polished.

Fortified

Solera System

Fractional blending across decades — one wine, perpetually renewed.

▼

What It Is

The solera system is used in Sherry and Madeira production. Wine is aged in a stack of barrels (called a "solera"). Every year, a fraction of the oldest wine (bottom barrel) is bottled and sold. It's replaced with slightly younger wine from the barrel above it, which is replaced by wine from the barrel above that, and so on. The cycle repeats endlessly.

Why It's Genius

A single bottle of solera-aged wine might contain drops from barrels ranging from 10 to 50+ years old — all fractionally blended together. This creates a consistency and depth impossible with single-vintage aging alone. It also means a solera is theoretically immortal: as long as you keep refreshing it, it never expires.

On Our List

Cossart Gordon's Bual Solera 1845 is a working solera that's been refreshed continuously since 1875. Every bottle contains drops from the original 1845 vintage, blended with newer wine — a living connection to 19th-century Madeira.

Fortified

Oxidative Aging

Letting wine breathe over years — building rancio and dried-fruit depth.

▼

What It Is

Most wines are protected from oxygen — it causes spoilage. Oxidative aging inverts this: barrels sit with an intentional air gap, and the wine is exposed to steady, controlled oxygen over months or years. The result is "maderization" — a shift from fresh fruit flavors to dried fruit, nuts, caramel, and toffee. The wine develops rancio complexity (nutty, spiced, savory edge).

The Paradox

Oxygen usually ruins wine. But at the right temperature, with the right alcohol level and acidity, oxidation becomes a flavor-building tool. Madeira's high acid keeps it from turning flat. Sherry's fortification (added spirit) preserves freshness even as oxidation transforms the flavor profile.

On Our List

Domaine de Rancy's Rivesaltes Ambré (2000) has spent 21 years in ambient cellar conditions, slowly oxidizing. Barbeito's Sercial and Malvasia Reserva Velha (1898 and 1834) are over a century old, their flavors transformed by decades of controlled oxidation into something otherworldly.

Dessert

Botrytis Cinerea (Noble Rot)

Fungus that concentrates sugar — nature's sweetness amplifier.

▼

What It Is

Botrytis cinerea is a fungus (mold) that attacks grape skins, piercing them and causing the grapes to shrivel. But unlike gray rot (which spoils wine), botrytis in the right conditions becomes "noble rot" — it dehydrates the grapes while concentrating the sugar, acidity, and flavors inside.

The Conditions

Botrytis only becomes noble under specific conditions: cool nights, morning fog, and afternoon sun. The fog creates humidity for the fungus to thrive; the sun evaporates water from the fruit, concentrating sugars. Change one variable and you get spoiled fruit instead of liquid gold. This is why Sauternes (morning fog off the Ciron river) and Tokaij (Hungarian valleys with similar morning mist) became the world's botrytis capitals.

The Harvest

Berries don't ripen evenly — botrytis takes them at different rates. Pickers must return to the vineyard repeatedly over weeks, sometimes even selecting individual berries rather than whole bunches. In poor years, if botrytis doesn't develop properly or acidity is lost, producers declassify the entire vintage rather than bottle substandard wine. This is why great Sauternes and Tokaij cost what they do.

On Our List

Château Rieussec (2009) and Château d'Yquem (1999) are both built on botrytis-affected grapes. The 1999 Yquem shows how beautifully botrytis ages — over 25 years on, it's still balanced, elegant, and nowhere near the end of its life.
